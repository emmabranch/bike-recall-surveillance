Signals

Is any kind of recall more common than its own recent history would predict?

How this works

Surveillance systems for infectious disease flag a count when it rises above what the recent past would predict. This page applies the same idea to US bicycle recalls:

  • Baseline. For each kind of recall and each year, the expected count is the average of the five previous years.
  • Threshold. A Poisson model gives the range of counts that chance alone would produce around that average. A count above the 95th percentile is marked Watch, and a count above the 99th percentile is marked Signal. Counts below 3 are never flagged, because one or two recalls are not a pattern.
  • The current year is incomplete, so it is compared year-to-date: recalls up to 12 November this year against the same part of each of the five previous years.

This is the logic of the historical limits method used in routine notifiable disease reporting, adapted to annual counts and small numbers.

What is flagged now

For 2025 and 2026 to date, the flagged series are: E-bike related (Signal: 10 against 2.8 expected, 2025); Other (Signal: 4 against 0.6 expected, 2026 to date); Fire or burn (Watch: 4 against 1 expected, 2025).

Across all 352 series-year comparisons since 2005, 11 are Signals and 10 are Watches. With this many comparisons, chance alone would produce a few flags (at most about 4 Signals and 14 Watches at these thresholds), so a single flag is a reason to look closer, not proof of a problem.

What a signal means here. A signal says a count is unusual for that series, not that products have become less safe. Recalls rise when a regulator focuses on a product type, when a new standard is introduced, or when one large company recalls several models at once. Each signal is a prompt to read the recalls behind it in the dashboard.
